---
title: Pitching Yeast
description: Choosing a pitch rate, building a starter, and managing the fermentation through to conditioning.
status: published
---

Pitch rate is the number of viable yeast cells added per millilitre of wort
per degree Plato. Underpitching makes the yeast work harder than it should:
the population has to grow further before it can begin fermenting in
earnest, and the stress of that growth phase throws off compounds the
drinker will eventually taste. Overpitching is a milder fault but not a free
one, since a population that barely has to reproduce produces a beer that
can read as thin and characterless.

The conventional figures are around three quarters of a million cells per
millilitre per degree Plato for a clean ale, and roughly double that for a
lager, which ferments colder and therefore slower. A single fresh pack of
liquid yeast holds well short of what a standard batch of ordinary strength
needs, which is why starters exist.

A starter is a small volume of low-gravity wort pitched with the pack and
left on a stir plate for a day or two. The yeast reproduces up to the
population the real batch requires. Because the aim is growth rather than
flavour, starter wort is made deliberately plain, and most brewers chill the
finished starter and decant the spent liquid rather than pitching it.

Temperature control through the fermentation itself matters as much as the
pitch. Most ale strains are happiest between eighteen and twenty degrees,
and a fermentation that runs away above that produces fusel alcohols that
taste hot and solvent-like.

Toward the end of fermentation the yeast turns to cleaning up after itself.
The compound that matters most here is diacetyl, which tastes strongly of
butter or butterscotch and is one of the most commonly recognised faults in
a finished beer. Yeast produces it as a by-product early on and then
reabsorbs it, but only while it is still active and still warm enough to
work.

The remedy is a diacetyl rest: rather than crashing the temperature as soon
as the gravity stabilises, raise it by two or three degrees for a couple of
days and leave the yeast in contact with the beer. The population scavenges
the diacetyl it made earlier and the buttery character disappears. Chilling
too early strands the compound in the beer, where nothing later in the
process will remove it.
